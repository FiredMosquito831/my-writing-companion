# Try-Fail Cycles and the Failure Ladder

<!-- Ideas-only: mechanism credited to Brandon Sanderson's BYU creative-writing lectures, Jim Butcher's convention-notes essays, and Writing Excuses episodes 10.29 and 21.14; no source text or code copied. All prose original. -->

The arc-level counterpart to the scene-level rubric in
`creative-writing-craft/resources/scene-and-sequel.md`. Scene-and-sequel flags
the tidy win *per scene*; this file governs the *arc*: how many attempts the
protagonist spends before the arc goal succeeds, and what each one costs. It
is the standard cure for the saggy middle (`story-architecture/structural-problems.md`
diagnoses it; this is the prescription).

## The failure ladder

For any arc with a protagonist goal: **two to three attempts before the goal
succeeds, each costing more than the last.** The first attempt fails or
half-fails; the protagonist adapts and pays; the last attempt succeeds
*because* of what the earlier attempts taught or cost — never because the
obstacle got tired. If the protagonist succeeds on the first real attempt,
there is no ladder, and the arc's middle has nothing to stand on.

The ladder is what the middle is *for*: not a sequence of hurdles, but a
sequence of prices.

## Attempt vs. barrier

Plot the two separately. The **barrier** is what stands between the
protagonist and the goal — the locked vault, the rival's claim, the law, the
wound. The **attempt** is what the protagonist does against it this time.
Weak middles usually reuse one barrier with cosmetic attempts (three
increasingly dramatic lock-picks); strong middles either change the attempt
against a fixed barrier or reveal the barrier was never the real one.

## Outcomes: no / yes, but / no, and

Every attempt ends in one of three plotting outcomes — never a tidy win:

- **No** — the attempt fails outright; the situation is worse by effort spent.
- **Yes, but** — the attempt succeeds *and* a new problem arrives (the vault
  opens; the vault was bait).
- **No, and** — the attempt fails *and* a new cost lands (the vault stays
  shut; the alarm tells the owner someone is here).

"Yes, but" and "no, and" are not scene-level punctuation here; they are how
each *rung* hands the arc to the next one. They are what keeps the goal-state
moving even while the goal stays fixed.

## Costs escalate

Each attempt costs more than the last. Early rungs spend comfort, money, or
time; middle rungs spend relationships, position, or safety; the final rung
costs whatever the protagonist still believed they could keep. Escalation —
not magnitude — is the requirement: a later rung that risks less than an
earlier one deflates the arc. This is what makes the eventual success feel
earned rather than scheduled.

## Failure builds empathy

Failures are where the reader learns who the protagonist is under pressure.
Competence without failure reads as a machine running; a failure paid
honestly — reacted to, absorbed, converted into a different next attempt —
buys the reader's loyalty for the climax. Let the protagonist be wrong in a
way characteristic of them.

## Make each try-fail do double duty

A rung should do two jobs: advance the arc goal *and* advance something else
— a second promise, a subplot, a relationship, a mystery — while revealing
character. A rung that only says "they tried and failed" is a rung to rewrite.
Double duty is why the ladder doesn't pad the middle: the rungs are the
middle's content, not filler between the real scenes.

## Anti-checklist warning

Two or three attempts is a **floor shape, not a formula**. The failure mode it
prescribes against is first-attempt success; it does not license padding. If
the middle becomes a rotation of three identical obstacles — same barrier,
same cost, same outcome, reskinned — you have built the saggy middle out of
compliant parts. Count nothing; escalate everything. Some arcs legitimately
have no ladder (tests passed, quiet interludes, arcs whose point is
unchangedness); forcing one there flattens them.

## How it is used

- **Outliner**: arc outlines show the ladder with escalating costs for any
  arc with a protagonist goal; first-attempt success is named as a finding
  for the author, not a verdict.
- **Critic**: diagnostic question — "did the protagonist succeed on the first
  real attempt at the arc's goal?" — at `suggestion` severity.
- **Muse**: when briefing the writer mid-arc, the state card's story position
  and the outline's remaining rungs say which price comes next.
