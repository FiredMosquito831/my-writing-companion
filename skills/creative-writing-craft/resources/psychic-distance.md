# Psychic Distance

Adapted from `calliope-editor/writing-skills/skills/craft/psychic-distance/SKILL.md` — MIT. Changes: extracted the five-rung ladder, the four reading markers, the filter-verb lever, and the common miss-patterns from the coaching-skill frame into a flat craft reference (no drills, no intake loop) so muse and critics can diagnose POV lurches and writers can control distance on purpose; source is the coaching skill, this is the reference it teaches from.

Psychic distance is how close the narration stands to a character's inner life — Gardner's five degrees, from a remote camera to full interiority. Control it deliberately: move closer for emotional peaks, character-defining moments, and interiority; pull back for transitions, time compression, and establishing context. The rhythm of close and far gives prose its emotional shape.

Default AI prose sits at a flat middle distance — close enough to seem personal, far enough to feel safe. Push past that. Go closer when it matters.

## The five rungs

- **Rung 1 — remote.** The wide shot: era, place, "a man," no inner life. ("It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of the doorway.")
- **Rung 2 — general.** A named character seen from outside, disposition summarized.
- **Rung 3 — closer.** A plain, direct statement of the character's feeling.
- **Rung 4 — close.** The character's own diction and rhythm bleed into the narration (free indirect discourse) — colloquialisms, an expletive, *without* quotes or a "he thought" tag.
- **Rung 5 — deep.** Fully inside: raw sensation, associative, the thought as it forms; fragments, and the cadence of a mind rather than a narrator. ("Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes, freezing and plugging up your miserable soul.")

## Read the rung off four markers — never one

1. **Naming** — impersonal epithet ("a large man") → full formal name → first name → bare pronoun → (deepest) even second person, "your."
2. **Interiority** — external action → a named preference → a stated emotion → emotion in the character's *own voice* → raw unmediated sensation.
3. **Free indirect discourse** — at rung 4 the character's words colour the narration with no quotation marks and no tag.
4. **Syntax** — fragments and present/second-person immediacy mark the deep rung.

Plus one countable lever: **filter verbs** — *saw, heard, felt, noticed, watched, realized, seemed, knew* — hold the reader one remove back. They are the most operational tell of a mid rung; delete the perceiving verb and render the thing directly, and the distance collapses closer.

## Two things held firm

- Distance is a **separate dial from grammatical person** — first person can sit at the *farthest* rung and a close third at the deepest; person is not closeness.
- Distance is **not** the show/tell binary — a far passage can still be vivid.

## POV-lurch diagnosis (common miss-patterns)

When POV breaks, the cause is usually one of these:

- **Distance drift** — the rung slips without the writer meaning it to; the most common defect.
- **The lurch** — a shift between rungs with no dramatic reason, jarring the reader. The fix is the staged pass-through: out to the neutral middle rungs, then back in.
- **Monotone** — the passage sits at one rung the whole way when the moment wants movement (distance is a tool meant to shift).
- **Filter fog** — "she saw / he felt / he noticed" stacking up and holding the reader at arm's length when the moment wants to be close.
- **Fake closeness** — the naming and syntax say "deep," but the *words* are still the narrator's, not the character's. At a deep rung the vocabulary must be the **character's**.
- **Head-hop** — the distance breaks by jumping into a *second* character's interiority with no hand-off. The fix is the staged pass-through, not "never change heads."

## How it is used

- **Muse** uses the ladder to annotate POV intent in scene briefs and to flag lurches for the author.
- **Critics** use it to diagnose POV breaks — a passage that drifts, lurches, or fakes closeness is a voice/structure finding.
- **Not a "closer is better" rule**: a wide, cool distance can be exactly right (omniscient narrator, ironic remove, grief held at arm's length). The deepest rung is strong medicine — Gardner reserves it for altered states, and most narration lives in the middle.

## The shelf

- **John Gardner**, *The Art of Fiction* — the origin of psychic distance, the five-degree ladder, and the "camera" map.
- **David Jauss** — distance as the real work of POV; closeness is "where the language is coming from," not grammatical person.
- **Emma Darwin**, *This Itch of Writing* — the staged POV hand-off through the neutral middle rungs.
- **Ursula K. Le Guin**, *Steering the Craft* — POV modes; the "filter" term is Chatman's, via Burroway.
