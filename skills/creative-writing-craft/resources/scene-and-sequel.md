# Scene and Sequel

Adapted from `calliope-editor/writing-skills/skills/craft/scene-architecture/SKILL.md` — MIT. Changes: extracted the scene/sequel rubric, the "therefore/but" seam, and the named failure patterns from the coaching-skill frame into a flat craft reference (no drills, no intake loop) so muse can annotate briefs and critics can diagnose flat scenes; source is the coaching skill, this is the reference it teaches from.

The default unit of proactive fiction. Not a law — a mood-setting interlude, a lyrical set piece, a deliberate breather may legitimately not chase a goal, and grading those as "broken" would be wrong — but most scenes that lose a reader are missing one of the parts below.

## A scene — the proactive unit (a character going after something)

- **Goal** — a clear, concrete thing the POV character wants *in this scene, now*. Not a life ambition: a scene-sized objective you could photograph.
- **Conflict** — real opposition to that goal, *escalating*: each obstacle costs more than the last.
- **Disaster** — the scene turns on a setback. The cleanest turns are **"no"** (they fail) or **"yes, but"** (they get it and it's worse) — never a tidy, frictionless win. The disaster is what makes you turn the page.

## A sequel — the reactive unit (a character absorbing what happened)

The half most writers skip. A disaster without a sequel makes the next goal feel arbitrary.

- **Reaction** — the felt, bodily response to the disaster.
- **Dilemma** — a genuine bad-options choice with no clean way out.
- **Decision** — the choice that becomes the *next* scene's goal.

## The seam

Scenes connect by **"therefore" or "but,"** never **"and then."** One unit should *cause* the next. (Trey Parker and Matt Stone's test — itself an echo of E. M. Forster's story-versus-plot: "the king died and then the queen died" is a story; "the king died *and the queen died of grief*" is a plot.) Every scene owes an answer to: *why does this matter, and why now?*

## Named failure patterns

When a scene reads flat, the cause is usually one of these (diagnostic, from the coaching source):

- **The errand** — a goal with no real opposition; the character just does the thing.
- **The tidy win** — the scene ends on frictionless success; no turn.
- **And-then** — beats in sequence with no causal "therefore / but" between them.
- **The skipped sequel** — a disaster with no reaction-dilemma-decision, so the next goal feels arbitrary.
- **The static scene** — no scene-goal at all; talking heads or description with nothing pursued.
- **The floating scene** — no answer to "why this, why now"; it could be cut with nothing lost.

## How it is used

- **Muse** uses the rubric to annotate scene briefs (in `work/outline/`) and to flag flat chapters for the author.
- **Critics** use it to diagnose flat scenes — a scene missing its disaster, or a chapter that is all sequel, is a structural finding.
- **Not a straitjacket**: a quiet mood-piece or lyrical set piece may legitimately run on sequel rhythm. The diagnostic question is whether the scene is *doing what it set out to do*, not whether it has a goal.

## The shelf

- **Dwight V. Swain**, *Techniques of the Selling Writer* — the source of scene and sequel and the goal-conflict-disaster spine.
- **Jack M. Bickham**, *Scene & Structure* — the "no" / "yes, but" disaster framing.
- **Robert McKee**, *Story* — the scene as a *turn* on a value charge, which sharpens why a frictionless scene reads as dead.
